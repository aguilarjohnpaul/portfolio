Ask an owner with 14 Google reviews why there aren't more, and you'll hear some version of "people don't leave reviews anymore." Then ask how the last 30 customers were asked. The answer is usually a line on the invoice, a sticker on the truck, or nothing, because the office manager was going to send something and the week got away from her.

The customers would have done it. The ask never reached them while they still cared.

That's the whole problem with reviews for most service businesses in San Antonio, and it's a different problem from the one owners think they have. They think they have a supply problem. They have a timing problem, and timing problems are fixable in a week.

## Google reads the dates, not just the stars

Before the ask, it's worth being clear about what reviews do, because "get more reviews" has become one of those things everyone says and nobody explains.

Your star rating matters less than you'd guess once you're above about 4.3. What Google weighs harder is how many reviews you have compared with the other shops it's considering for the same search, and how recently the last ones came in. A profile with 60 reviews and nothing new since spring loses ground every month to a profile with 35 and three from the last two weeks. Google reads a steady trickle as a business that's open, working, and still making people happy. It reads a long gap as a question mark.

Searchers read it the same way, which is the part owners feel directly. Someone comparing three tree services on a Saturday morning taps the reviews and scrolls to the dates. Two years ago. Eighteen months ago. She doesn't know if you still have the same crew. The shop with a review from last Tuesday wins the call, even with a lower average.

So the number that matters isn't your total. It's your last 90 days against the three businesses above you on the map. Most owners have never looked at it that way, and when they do, the gap is usually smaller than they feared and more fixable than they assumed.

## A review is a favor, and favors expire

What the invoice sticker gets wrong is simple. A review is a small favor a customer does for you, and the willingness to do a favor peaks at a specific moment and decays fast.

Picture a tree job in Alamo Heights. The crew drops a dead live oak that's been scaring the homeowner every storm season for three years. By 4 in the afternoon the yard is raked, the stump is ground, and she's standing in the driveway looking at the sky where the tree used to be. Right then she'd tell anyone how good you are. She'd write a paragraph.

Tomorrow she'd still do it, if it were easy. By the weekend she's thinking about something else. When the invoice arrives a week later with "Review us on Google" at the bottom, she means to, and it goes in the stack.

The ask that works lands in that driveway moment, or within a day of it. The ask that doesn't work is the one that lands when the paperwork happens to be ready, which is a schedule built around your office, not her gratitude.

I'll concede the obvious thing here. Some trades have it harder than others. A landscaper sees the same customer every month and has a dozen chances. A roofer sees a homeowner once every 15 years, and that homeowner is writing a big check for something she can't see from the ground, which is not a mood that produces glowing paragraphs. The roofer's review rate will always be lower. But the roofer's competitors have the same problem, so the shop that asks at the right moment still pulls ahead. The comparison is never against perfect. It's against the three guys above you.

## Who asks matters as much as when

The second thing most shops get wrong is who does the asking.

A text from the business line that says "Thanks for choosing us, please review us here" is an ask from a company. A customer feels no obligation to a company. An ask from the person who was in her yard, by name, is an ask from a human being she just spent the afternoon with, and people say yes to people.

That's uncomfortable for a lot of owners, because it means the crew lead or the tech has to be part of it, and they'd rather keep the crew focused on the work. But the crew is the only part of your business the customer actually met. A tech who says "If you're happy with how this turned out, it'd help me a lot if you left a few words on Google, I'll have the office text you the link" converts at a rate no automated message will touch. The office text is the follow-through. The tech is the ask.

The third piece is friction, and this one is mechanical. Every step between the customer's yes and the published review loses people. Having to search for your business name loses some. A link that opens the wrong page loses more. A link that asks her to sign in to an account she doesn't remember loses most of the rest. The link that works opens the review box directly, on her phone, in one tap. If you don't know whether yours does that, send it to yourself and count the taps.

## Platforms will sell you a fix for the wrong problem

The review software market exists because of all this, and some of it is good. But notice what it sells. Automated messages, drip sequences, dashboards. Tools for a supply problem. If your ask is late and comes from a company, automating it gets you a late ask from a company, sent on time.

Google itself has mixed motives here too. It wants more reviews on the platform and it will nudge you to get them. It also suspends profiles that get a burst of reviews from the same address, or reviews that read like they were written by the same hand, or anything that looks like a reward was offered. The platform wants volume and punishes shortcuts, and it won't tell you which side of that line you're on until you're on the wrong side of it.

Which leaves the unglamorous answer. The fix is a habit, not a subscription. Decide who asks, decide when, make the link one tap, and reply to every review in a sentence or two, because searchers read your replies as a preview of what it's like to call you.

## The two-minute check

Open your Google Business Profile on your phone and sort the reviews by newest. Write down the dates of the last ten.

Now think about how many jobs you finished in that stretch. If the last ten reviews cover eight months and you did 120 jobs in those eight months, roughly one customer in twelve is leaving a review, and that's a broken ask, not a bad customer base. If the last ten cover six weeks, your ask is working and the question is whether it's working better than the shops above you.

Then do the same for the top three businesses on the map for your main search. Their last ten dates, next to yours. That comparison tells you more about where you stand than your total ever will.

## What this changes

If your last ten reviews are spread across most of a year, don't buy the software yet. Change who asks and when. Put the ask in the driveway, from the person who did the work, with a one-tap link behind it. Give it 30 days and count again. That one change usually does more than anything else a shop can do to its profile in a month, and it costs nothing but a conversation with the crew.

If your cadence is already steady and you're still stuck below the same three shops, reviews aren't your bottleneck, and someone telling you to "get more reviews" is selling you the generic answer. The gap is somewhere else on the profile or in your listings, and that's a different conversation.
