Businesses that ask for a review after every job get one from roughly one customer in four. Businesses that wait get one a quarter, usually from a friend. The whole system is three habits: ask at the right moment with a link that works on a phone, make the ask part of a step you already do, and reply to every review within two business days.

## Why do reviews matter more than almost anything else on the profile?

Google uses three things about your reviews to rank you in the map results: how many you have, your average rating, and how recently the latest ones came in. Searchers use the same three to choose. A plumber with 4.8 stars from 90 reviews, three of them this month, beats one with 4.9 from 20 reviews and the last one in March, both in the ranking and in the customer's head.

Recency is the part most owners miss. A pile of reviews from the year you opened goes stale. Google and customers both read "the last review was 11 months ago" as "something changed."

## When is the right moment to ask?

At the moment of praise. That's usually right after the job, when the customer says "that looks great" or "thanks for coming out so fast." Not a week later from the office. The feeling fades, and so does the response rate.

For trades with a clear finish (a repair, an install, a delivery), the ask goes out the same day, within an hour if you can. For long projects (a remodel, a landscape install), ask at the walkthrough, when the client is standing in the finished room.

If there was a problem on the job, fix the problem first and ask on the next clean one. Asking after a miss earns the review you don't want.

## What exactly do I say?

Short, personal, with the link. By text:

> Thanks for having us out today, Maria. If you have 30 seconds, a Google review helps the next homeowner find us: [link]. Either way, thanks for the work.

By email, the same thing with one more line. By QR code on the invoice or a card in the box: "How did we do? Scan to leave a Google review." The link should open the review box directly. Google gives you that link in your profile under "Ask for reviews."

Two prompts raise the quality of what people write: "What job did we do?" and "What stood out?" Reviews that name the service ("replaced our water heater") help you rank for that service.

Don't ask for five stars. Ask for a review. Don't ask only the customers you think were happy. Google's policy and the FTC both prohibit steering and incentives, and a profile of nothing but glowing reviews with no replies reads as staged anyway.

## How do I make the ask happen without remembering it?

Attach it to a step you already do. Every job already ends with an invoice, a payment, a text saying you're done, or a walkthrough. The review ask rides along with one of those.

If you invoice from QuickBooks, Jobber, Housecall Pro, ServiceTitan or Square, there's a place to add the review link to the invoice or the "job complete" message. If you text customers from your phone, save the message as a text replacement so it's two taps. If you hand over a paper invoice, print the QR code on it.

Pick one. The best review system is the one that fires without you thinking about it.

## What do I say back?

Reply to every review, within two business days, in your own voice. Thirty words is plenty.

For a good review: thank them, name the job, mention something specific. "Thanks, Carlos. Glad the new panel took care of the breaker trips. Enjoy the garage fridge." The next reader learns what you did and that you remember your customers.

For a bad review: calm, short, specific, and take it offline. "Hi Dana, I'm sorry the crew ran late on Tuesday. That's on us. I'd like to make it right; please call me at (737) 391-3041." Never argue, never reveal private details, never write a paragraph. The reply is for the hundred people who'll read it, not the one who wrote it.

For a review you believe is fake or breaks Google's rules (never a customer, a competitor, spam), flag it from your profile and reply anyway, briefly: "We don't have a record of working with you. If this is a mistake, please call us so we can sort it out."

## What results should I expect?

A business that goes from no system to asking after every job typically sees its first new reviews within two weeks and a steady pace within two months: roughly one review for every four jobs where the ask went out. A shop doing 40 jobs a month lands around 10 reviews a month, which in most San Antonio trades passes the competition inside a quarter.

Your average rating usually goes up, not down, because the silent happy majority finally shows up. If you're worried that asking everyone invites bad reviews, the math says otherwise: unhappy customers were already leaving reviews. You're adding the happy ones.
