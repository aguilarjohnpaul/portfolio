Ask an owner who pays someone for local SEO what that person did last month, and the most common answer is a pause, then "they send a report." Ask what the report said, and it's another pause.

That's not the owner's fault. The whole arrangement is built so you won't ask. The report arrives, it has charts, the charts mostly go up, and you have a business to run. The person on the other end is counting on exactly that.

I want to make the case that the money is the wrong thing to focus on when you're deciding whether a local search person is worth keeping. What matters is whether the work is happening, and the work is specific enough that you can check. Once you know what it looks like, the report either shows it or it doesn't.

## The work is boring, and that's how you know it's real

Local search for a single-location service business in San Antonio isn't mysterious. It's a short list of repetitive things done every month without fail.

Someone logs into your Google Business Profile and changes something. Adds the photos from last week's jobs. Updates the services list when you start offering something new. Posts about the storm damage work you're doing this month. Answers the questions strangers leave on the profile before a competitor does. Someone makes sure the review ask went out after every job and writes a reply to every review that came in, in a human voice, within a few days. Someone checks the ten listings that Google cross-references and fixes the drift. Every quarter or so, someone adds a page to your website for a service or a part of town you're not showing up for yet.

That's it. That's the job. It's not clever. It's a habit, kept on your behalf by someone who won't forget it the way you would in the middle of a busy season.

Which means it leaves tracks. Every one of those actions is visible. The profile shows when it was last edited. Reviews show replies and dates. Posts have timestamps. Pages have publish dates. A person doing the work can show you, for any given month, exactly what they touched. A person not doing it can show you a chart.

## The five numbers a report should show

Reports are where the industry hides. A report full of impressions, rankings for keywords you've never heard of, and a domain authority score tells you almost nothing about whether the phone is ringing more than it was. Those numbers are chosen because they go up easily and because you can't argue with them.

Five numbers tell you what's actually happening, and all five come straight from Google.

How many calls came from the profile this month. How many people asked for directions. How many clicked through to the website from the profile. How many reviews you have now compared with last month. And where you sit in the map for the three or four searches you actually care about, from the neighborhoods you actually work, not from a data center in Virginia.

Those five, month over month, on one page. If a report has them, the person is at least measuring the right things. If it has 30 numbers and none of those five, it's a document designed to look like work.

I'll grant that some months there is nothing to change. The profile is right, the reviews are coming in, the listings are clean. A good person tells you that in a sentence and the five numbers hold steady. That's fine. What isn't fine is a month where nothing changed and the report doesn't say so, because the report was never about telling you what happened.

## Where the retainers go wrong

The failure modes are predictable, because the business model pushes toward them.

The first is the agency that's really a software subscription. You're paying for a dashboard and an automated posting tool that puts a stock photo on your profile every Tuesday. Nobody has looked at your actual profile in months. The posts are there, the report shows "12 posts published," and the content is the same thing it posts for the roofer in Houston.

The second is the agency that does the setup and then coasts. The first two months are real. The profile gets fixed, the listings get cleaned, the numbers jump. Then the retainer continues and the work doesn't, because the hard part is done and the owner is happy with the jump. Six months later the review cadence has died and nobody noticed.

The third is the agency that's measuring itself. Rankings for 200 keywords, most of which nobody in San Antonio searches. Impressions, which count every time your name flickered on a screen whether anyone saw it. Website traffic that turns out to be bots. All of it up and to the right, none of it connected to a call.

What these have in common is that you can't tell from the outside, and the report is built so you won't try. The platforms play along. Google's own dashboard emphasizes views and impressions over calls, because big numbers make the profile feel valuable, and a profile that feels valuable is one you'll buy ads on.

## One question that sorts it out

You don't need to understand any of this to find out whether your person is doing the work. Ask them one thing, in writing, and watch what comes back.

"What did you change on my profile, my listings or my website last month, and on what dates?"

A person doing the work answers in ten minutes with a short list. Added six photos on the 4th, replied to three reviews on the 9th and 22nd, fixed the Yelp phone number on the 15th, published the drain cleaning page on the 28th. It's not impressive. It's specific, and it's checkable, and that's the whole point.

A person not doing the work sends you the report again. Or a paragraph about strategy. Or asks to schedule a call to walk you through the results. Any of those is the answer.

Ask it before the next invoice, not after. And if you're the one doing the work yourself, ask it of yourself at the end of the month. The honest answer is usually "nothing since July," which is useful to know.

## What this changes

If you get the list back and it's real, keep paying. You've got one of the good ones, and they're worth more than the amount on the invoice, whatever it is. Ask for the five numbers on one page if you're not already getting them, and stop reading the rest of the report.

If you get a report back instead of a list, you've learned what you're buying. Don't argue about it.
